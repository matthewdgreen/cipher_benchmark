# Moustier Church Altar Cryptograms — Source Note

## Physical Location

Two cryptograms are carved into the side altars of **Saint-Martin's Church, Moustier (Frasnes-lez-Anvaing), eastern Belgium**, near the French border. The Virgin's Altar is positioned left of the main altar; the St. Martin's Altar is symmetrically to the right.

The altars date from the church's 1838–1843 reconstruction, following structural collapse. The cryptograms were almost certainly carved at that time.

## Transcription Sources

Three independently reported transcriptions of the altar inscriptions are in circulation:

1. **Klaus Schmeh (2017)** — direct observation during a 2015 visit, published in the "Top 50 Unsolved Encrypted Messages" series:
   - URL: https://scienceblogs.de/klausis-krypto-kolumne/2017/11/07/the-top-50-unsolved-encrypted-messages-18-the-moustier-altar-inscriptions/
   - Considered the most authoritative modern transcription.

2. **Cipher Mysteries (Nick Pelling, 2013)** — blog series with transcription contributed by direct observer Menno Knul:
   - https://ciphermysteries.com/2013/04/02/the-moustier-church-cryptograms
   - https://ciphermysteries.com/2013/07/12/paul-de-saint-hilaires-notes-on-moustier-church
   - Notes variable letter heights, inconsistent serifs, and possible multi-craftsperson execution.

3. **AZdecrypt bundled cipher** — a widely circulated community transcription:
   - `Ciphers/Unsolved/Moustier St Martin.txt` and `Moustier Virgin.txt`
   - Minor discrepancy from sources 1–2: the Virgin transcription omits a `[` character in line 5 (`LFN^BKP` vs `LFN^[BKP`).

The transcriptions stored here follow the Schmeh/Cipher Mysteries versions, which are based on direct inspection of the physical inscriptions.

## Character Set

- Standard Latin uppercase letters A–Z
- `^` (caret) — used as a distinct cipher symbol; not an accent modifier
- `[` (left bracket) — used as a distinct cipher symbol; possibly a composite glyph (Γ+L?)

Letter frequencies across both inscriptions are similar, consistent with a single monoalphabetic substitution cipher applied to a European language (most likely French or Latin).

## Attempted Decipherment

- **Professor Jean Connart** researched the cryptograms from 1961 without conclusive results.
- **Rudy Cambier** claimed a decipherment in December 1995 via an abjad (consonant-only) hypothesis, suggesting the ten pairs of lines encode the Ten Commandments. His interpretation is not widely accepted.
- Documented in: **NSA Cryptolog** (September 1974, declassified) as a then-unsolved mystery.
- Referenced in: **Paul de Saint-Hilaire**, *La Belgique Mystérieuse* (1973), though without primary sources.
